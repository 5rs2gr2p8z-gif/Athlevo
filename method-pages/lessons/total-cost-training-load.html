Total cost: why training feels harder than the plan says

By Dean Castro, Founder and Head Coach at Athlevo. Updated 14 November 2026. 3 minute read.

Most plans assume you sleep seven to nine hours, keep a steady work schedule, run in mild weather and recover fully between sessions. Most working adults do not live like that. The Athlevo total cost framework counts everything that draws on your recovery, not just the running.

In short

  • Adapting to training has a cost, and the cost is paid across your whole life.
  • Athlevo reads four loads: training, environmental, life and structural.
  • Tiredness that never clears is usually a load accounting problem, not a motivation problem.

  1. A cost on every system

A cost on every system

In stress research, allostatic load is the cumulative wear and tear on the body from repeated or chronic stress. Athlevo borrows the idea for training. Adapting to a block of work has a cost, and that cost builds up across all the stress in your life, not only in your legs.

Runners often arrive describing the same pattern:

  • Low-grade tiredness that never fully clears.
  • Stop-start training: hard weeks, a breakdown, a restart.
  • High effort with little to show for it.
  • Aches that build up without a single clear cause.

Athlevo's diagnosis is that this is not a motivation problem. It is a load accounting problem. The fix is to count the full cost, not only the training.

The four load inputs

1. Training load

Three things are read:

  • Acute load: the last 3 to 7 days. Total volume, intensity mix and how closely sessions are packed.
  • Chronic load: the last 4 to 6 weeks. Someone coming off a consistent high-volume block carries a different base than someone who has been sporadic.
  • Load balance: how the recent week compares with the recent weeks before it. A week well above your recent norm is treated as breakdown risk, even if it does not look extreme on paper.

2. Environmental load

Heat and humidity are a physiological cost, not scenery. See running in the heat.

3. Life load

Work stress, sleep quality, emotional strain, relationship demands, commute and food quality all draw on the same pool of recovery. Athlevo treats them as inputs, not as soft factors to note and ignore. A coach uses four quick questions:

  • Sleep over the last 3 nights: normal, disrupted or very poor.
  • Work stress this week: normal, elevated or high.
  • Energy on waking: good, flat or very low.
  • Anything unusual, such as travel, illness or emotional stress: yes or no.

4. Structural load

Where you are in a block decides how much tissue fatigue you carry.

  • Early block (weeks 1 to 3): structural load is low. Tissue is fresh.
  • Mid-block (weeks 4 to 7): it builds. This is where many runners feel the grind, not because sessions got harder but because the load is cumulative.
  • Peak weeks: structural load is highest and the most adaptation happens, but only if recovery weeks follow.
  • Deload weeks: structural load clears and the adaptation settles.

From four inputs to one decision

Once the four inputs are read, the question is how much training you can absorb today without hurting next week. That is your available capacity, and it decides whether a planned session goes ahead. See the Conversion Run.
