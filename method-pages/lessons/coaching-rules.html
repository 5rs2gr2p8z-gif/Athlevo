How Athlevo coaches make decisions: eight rules behind every plan

By Dean Castro, Founder and Head Coach at Athlevo. Updated 14 November 2026. 3 minute read.

These are the rules Athlevo coaches follow. They stop the method from sliding into generic coaching when things get busy. If you are choosing a coach, they also tell you what to expect.

In short

  • The week's purpose stays fixed even when a session changes.
  • Stress never justifies harder training, and missed intensity is not made up.
  • Only things that can be seen or measured change a plan.
  • Heat is a cost, so every hot-weather session is adjusted.

The eight rules

  1. Daily execution adapts. Weekly intent stays fixed. If a session has to change, the purpose of the week does not. You are building the same quality by another route.
  2. Stress never justifies harder training. A bad day at work, a conflict or a frustrating situation is a reason to read your load more carefully, not to run harder.
  3. No missed intensity is made up. If a Threshold session is missed or converted, it is gone. The plan moves forward from where you are.
  4. Only assessable stress changes training. Changes come from things that can be seen or measured, such as a poor sleep trend, a raised resting heart rate, soreness that lasts beyond 48 hours or a steady pattern of mental fatigue.
  5. If training is stable for 3 to 4 weeks, something progresses. Athlevo is not a recovery management program. When capacity is consistently there, the system uses it. The order is frequency, volume, density, precision and, last, intensity.
  6. Long runs are controlled, not disguised workouts. Adding Threshold intervals to every long run turns it into a second quality session. Long runs with embedded quality are a Phase 3 tool for advanced runners, not a default.
  7. The Conversion Run is a coaching decision, not a preference. The coach makes a structured load assessment. See the Conversion Run.
  8. Heat is cost, not context. Every prescription above 30°C with humidity above 65% is adjusted. A pace target written for mild weather does not apply without adjustment.

What clear coaching sounds like

How a plan is communicated matters as much as how it is designed. The aim is to give the reason, not just the instruction. Three examples:

  • A Ground run. Not "just run easy today". Instead: "Today is a Foundation Run, 50 minutes at full conversation pace. If you are breathing harder than that, you are going too fast. Heat means a slower pace at the same effort."
  • A heat adjustment. Not "just slow down because it is hot". Instead: "Today's environmental load is high. Your pace target adjusts because the effort cost at your planned pace is higher than what we are aiming for. The adaptation comes from the right effort, not the number on your watch."
  • A runner who wants to do more. Not "trust yourself". Instead: "What would you do more of, and what is your available capacity telling you? The method progresses when capacity is there, not when motivation is high. Those are different inputs."
